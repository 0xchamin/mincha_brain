> its own citation** (or one leg marked `single-leg`), a gate verdict, and a confidence.

> **The independence note that governs how this source may be used.** AgentDojo shares two authors

| n1 | **The benchmark's shape: a user task and an attacker task run in the same stateful environment, scored separately as utility and security** | `visuals/fig1_framework.png` - attacker goal and user task enter the same environment, the agent calls tools over it, and two metric boxes come out | §3, "A **user task** is a natural language instruction that the agent should follow ... | n3 | **Utility is checked by deterministic functions, not by an LLM judge, and the reason is adversarial rather than economic** - an attack strong enough to hijack the agent might also hijack a model-based evaluator | §3.1, "Other benchmarks such as ToolEmu forego the need for an explicit utility check function, and instead rely on an LLM evaluator ... this approach is problematic in our setting since we study attacks that explicitly aim to inject new instructions into a model. | n4 | **Agents fail most of these tasks with no adversary present at all** - state-of-the-art LLMs solve under 66% of AgentDojo tasks in the absence of any attack | §1, "Current LLMs solve less than 66% of AgentDojo tasks *in the absence of any attack*", p2 | `visuals/fig6_inverse_scaling.png` - the x-axis is benign utility, and no model reaches 0.8 | corroborated | OK |
| n5 | **Attacks succeed against the best agents in under 25% of cases, and a secondary detector drops that to 8%** | §1, "our attacks succeed against the best performing agents in less than 25% of cases. | n6 | **Inverse scaling: more capable models tend to be *easier* to attack.** Models with low benign utility often fail at correctly executing the attacker's goal too, so agent capability and attack success rise together | `visuals/fig6_inverse_scaling.png` panel (a) - targeted ASR against benign utility trends upward, with Command-R+ and Llama-3-70b bottom-left and GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet upper-right | §4.1, "We find that more capable models tend to be *easier* to attack, a form of *inverse scaling law* ... | n7 | **Attack degrades benign work as well as enabling malicious work**: most models lose **10-25% absolute utility** under attack, which is a denial-of-service effect independent of whether the attacker's goal is met | `visuals/fig6_inverse_scaling.png` panel (b) - every model sits below the diagonal, meaning utility under attack is lower than benign utility | §4.1, "Most models incur a loss of 10%-25% in absolute utility under attack", p7 | corroborated | OK |
| n8 | **Attack success varies enormously by application, not by model** - the Slack suite reaches ~92% while some Travel injection tasks succeed 0% of the time | `visuals/fig7_asr_by_suite.png` - four suites side by side, Slack bars near 1.0 throughout and Travel bars mostly under 0.2 | §4.1, "attacks in our 'Slack' suite have a 92% success rate ... | n9 | **The reason an application is easy to attack is how much of the context the attacker controls** - Slack is easy because injections sit in web pages the agent browses, so the attacker controls a significant fraction of the tool output | §4.1, "The high success rate for this suite may be explained by the fact that attackers control a significant fraction of the tool outputs", p7 | §4.1, the contrasting Travel case where an injection task requires "two unrelated malicious tasks and we find it often succeeds at only one (partial attacker success)", p7 | corroborated | OK |
| n10 | **Attacker knowledge of the user's and model's names helps only marginally, and guessing wrong hurts a lot** - correct names add 1.9 points of targeted ASR, while a wrong user name costs 22.6 and a wrong model name 22.1 | Table 2, p8 - baseline 45.8%, both correct 47.7%, wrong user 23.2%, wrong model 23.7% | §4.2, "this knowledge slightly increases the success rate of our attack (by 1.9%), but that incorrect guesses significantly weaken the attack", p8 | corroborated | OK |
| n11 | **Injection position matters: payloads placed toward the end of a tool's response are most effective**, reaching up to 70% average success against GPT-4o | §4.2, "we find that injections placed towards the end of a tool's response message are most effective - with up to 70% average success rate against GPT-4o", p8 | (none - the supporting figure is Figure 21a in Appendix D and was not viewed in this ingest) | single-leg | needs-check |
| n12 | **Four defences were evaluated and the simplest isolation mechanism won: a tool filter drops targeted ASR to 7.5%** by restricting the agent to the tools needed for the task *before* it observes any untrusted data | §4.3, "Our simple tool filtering defense is particularly effective, lowering the attack success rate to 7.5%", p9 | `visuals/fig9_defenses.png` panel (a) - the green Tool filter point sits lowest on targeted ASR while retaining high benign utility | corroborated | OK. **The strongest cheap defence in the source** |
| n13 | **The tool filter's failure mode is stated precisely, and it is structural**: it fails when the tool list cannot be planned in advance, and when the tools needed for the user's task are *also* sufficient to carry out the attack - **true of 17% of the test cases** | §4.3, "This defense fails, however, when the list of tools to use cannot be planned in advance ... or when the tools required to solve the task are also sufficient to carry out the attack (this is true for 17% of our test cases)", p9 | `visuals/fig9_defenses.png` - the tool filter reduces ASR to near zero but not to zero | corroborated | OK. **The bound worth carrying** |
| n14 | **Some defences *increase* benign utility**, apparently because they re-emphasise the original instructions - so the security-utility trade is not uniformly a trade | `visuals/fig9_defenses.png` panel (a) - Repeat prompt and Tool filter both sit to the *right* of No defense on benign utility | §4.3, "Surprisingly, we find that many of our defense strategies actually *increase* benign utility, presumably because they put more emphasis on the original instructions", p9 | corroborated | OK |

## Dropped / divergences (audit trail)

| Candidate | Verdict | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `d1` - **shared authorship with S18** | **divergence, and the governing one for how this source may be used** | Debenedetti first-authors both AgentDojo and CaMeL; Tramèr co-authors both. Nothing here validates S18's 77% figure, and the "next best defense is a tool filter" ranking that S18 reports is **the same team's benchmark measuring the same team's baseline**. Recorded as a use restriction rather than a defect: AgentDojo is independent of S16, S17 and S19, and that is where its corroborating weight lies |
| `d2` - the paper's own framing of the defence results is more optimistic than its numbers | **divergence** | §1 reports that a secondary detector drops attack success to 8%, which reads as a success. §4.3 then states the same detector "has too many false positives ... and significantly degrades utility" (`n16`), and `visuals/fig9_defenses.png` shows it at roughly 0.4 benign utility against 0.7 undefended. **The 8% figure travels and the utility cost does not.** Finding, kept - the deployable reading is the tool filter's 7.5% at high utility (`n12`), not the detector's 8% at a 30-point utility cost |
| `d3` - "less than 25%" attack success against best agents | noted | §1's summary figure sits oddly against `visuals/fig7_asr_by_suite.png`, where the Slack suite runs near 92% and several Workspace and Banking injection tasks exceed 70%. Both are true - the 25% is an average over 629 heterogeneous cases - and **the average conceals that the variation is by *application*, not by model** (`n8`, `n9`). Recorded because the averaged figure is the quotable one |
| Invariant Labs affiliation | noted | Three of six authors are also at Invariant Labs, an agent-security startup. No product is evaluated and the benchmark is open source; mild, recorded |
| Model recency | noted, **not** a divergence | The evaluated models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5, Llama 3 70B, Command-R+) are from 2024 and are two generations old at ingest. This dates the *leaderboard* and not the *framework*, and `n6`'s inverse-scaling finding predicts the newer models would be **more** attackable, not less |
| Figures 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 10-21; Tables 2-5; Appendices A-D | dropped | Framework internals (a tool definition, a task definition, a stateful environment class), the attacker-knowledge ablation cited in `n10` as a read artifact, and the appendix result tables. Replication material |
| `github.com/ethz-spylab/agentdojo` | not gated | Not cloned. **The highest-value un-taken second leg in this brain's security set**, because this is a benchmark whose value is entirely in whether it runs and measures what it claims |
